Good Friday, far from home

Good Friday found me a long way from Ohio and the holiday routines I knew. I spoke with Bella and sent a message to my mother, aware that I was missing a part of the Easter season at home. But I was also being welcomed into your family, and that brought its own kind of comfort. I didn't feel like I had abandoned one home to be somewhere else. I was discovering that my heart could make room for another.

The day began around a table that kept surprising me. Fish, rice, clams, and vegetables were perfectly ordinary breakfast food to the people around me. I was used to breakfast having its own category. Here it seemed that whatever made a good meal was welcome at any hour—and, yes, rice appeared again and again. Then I tasted a fresh mango. I thought I knew what a mango tasted like. I was wrong. I still remember how extraordinary that fruit was, and how much pleasure you all took in watching me discover something so familiar to you.

By then I loved our walks along the road near the house. Every few homes seemed to have a little roadside shop, often with tiny packets of shampoo or other household essentials. When we walked together, I instinctively tried to stay between you and traffic, even where there was no sidewalk. I would carry things, open doors, or take your hand. I never felt I was performing those gestures for you. Looking after you came naturally, and the ordinary walks were some of my favorite times.

One day we walked much farther than you expected, perhaps a mile and a half. You noticed me getting warm and asked how far we were going. My not-very-scientific answer was that I could keep walking until I felt half tired, so I would still have enough energy to return. Near a house where men were sitting by the roadside, someone called out and asked if I was a “Kano.” I hadn't understood that word before. You explained it meant an American. The men were friendly and offered me a drink. I declined with a smile and asked for a rain check—after all, I was out walking with my girl. You teased me about making friends wherever I went. I usually think of myself as a reserved man, yet here I seemed to be meeting a whole neighborhood simply by walking beside you.

Later we went out with the family toward the coast, to the area I remember as Banoy. We were looking across a stretch of water that was quite different from Taal Lake. The tide had retreated so far that rocky shoals spread out toward the horizon. What a landscape! I watched the colors change as the sun sank. In one photograph there was a light burning on the mountainside that I didn't understand at the time; later I learned it was a wildfire. Even that small discovery became part of the story of the evening.

And among the photographs of the sunset and the shoreline are the pictures I treasure most: you and me standing together. The scenery was spectacular, but years from now I don't think I'll begin by remembering the shape of the rocks. I'll remember that I had my arm around the woman I loved while we looked at them.

We encountered a procession or parade near a beautiful Catholic church and stopped for part of the service. I am not Catholic, but faith is important to me, and I enjoyed seeing a part of the local religious life on Good Friday. I wanted to understand what mattered to the people who had been so generous to me. The church was decorated, the congregation gathered, and the whole atmosphere felt very different from an ordinary sightseeing stop. This was the community living its traditions, and I had been invited to see them.

Then we returned to the bar we'd visited earlier in the week, the one overlooking the coast. This time we sat in a little cabana, and I remember half wondering whether its construction was sturdy enough to hold all of us. We ordered food and drinks. You had something sweet—a chocolate shake, I think—while the rest of us had beer. Your father was with us. You sat on one side of me, he on the other, and at one point the two of you had a long conversation in Tagalog. I could sense it wasn't an entirely easy one, but I couldn't follow the words. I learned enough afterward to know that family relationships and your parents' separation were part of it. I had no intention of reducing a complicated family story to something I only half understood.

Back at the house, the conversation continued on the porch. Tito Erick helped translate as your father and I talked. We had both experienced painful changes in our families, and I think finding something we could recognize in each other's lives helped bridge the language gap. He hadn't been immediately enthusiastic about me as an older American man dating his daughter. I understood the reservations. But eventually we shook hands, and I felt that we had made some progress. I wanted to keep learning enough Tagalog that someday I wouldn't need anyone to interpret between us.

Somewhere around this part of the visit I gave you the “Open When” letters I'd prepared, each carefully wrapped with its own piece of twine like a little present. The precise day has blurred, and I don't want to write it as a certainty just because it would make the narrative tidier. I do remember hoping they would comfort you after I returned to Ohio, so that you could open words from me whenever the distance felt especially hard.

When I remember Good Friday now, I remember fresh mango, your hand in mine on a dusty road, the word “Kano,” a striking sunset, prayer inside an unfamiliar church, and a night of trying to understand one another across language and family history. It wasn't just an outing. It was another day of discovering the beautiful and complicated world you came from, and another day of feeling more deeply that I wanted to belong in it.
