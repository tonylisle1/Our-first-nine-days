The goodbye I never wanted

We woke around five in the morning. I suggested that we brush our teeth and get cleaned up, and then we went outside for a little quiet time together. There was a sitting area near the beach with large cube-shaped cushions. We settled in and talked until we were told we had to move because the spot was intended for photographs. We laughed about that and went looking for coffee. I wish I could have made those ordinary minutes last much longer.

That morning every small routine carried a different weight. Coffee, finding a place to sit, walking outside, watching the beach: these were the same kinds of ordinary moments I'd treasured all week, except now I knew they were the last ones for a while. We found that little outdoor seating area with the big cube-shaped cushions and settled in together. We were asked to move because it was meant as a photo area. We laughed and went to look for coffee instead. Even that interruption became one more thing I wanted to remember.

Breakfast was Jollibee—chicken and spaghetti in the morning, another reminder that what seemed normal here could still surprise the American in me. We swam again and tried to enjoy the time without letting the approaching flight fill every conversation. But I could feel the hours being counted. Packing, traveling back, organizing the luggage: each ordinary task was a step closer to the separation we'd talked about for months.

During the trip I had discovered a habit I never wanted to lose. Sometimes while we lay together I would trace my fingers along your arm or gently move your hair, watching the movement as if I could memorize the feeling of your skin. It was almost a trance. I knew I would soon return to a screen, a call, a photograph, and the memory of a touch instead of the touch itself. That is why the final drive and the final goodbye were so difficult. It wasn't that I doubted us. I knew I was leaving someone who had come to feel like home.

The day was full of little lasts. We had Jollibee delivered—chicken and spaghetti for breakfast, something that would still make me laugh at home—and I went outside to take pictures of the suite, the balcony, and the stretch of beach beyond it. I wasn't just documenting a building. I wanted to remember where we had woken up together on our last morning. We swam once more before it was time to shower, pack, and head back to the family house. The photographs of the balcony and the sea are reminders that the final day began in sunshine, even though I could already feel the goodbye approaching.

Back at the house, packing was harder than unpacking had been. It felt as if every zipper pulled me one step closer to the airport. I gave you the necklace and earrings I had brought for your approaching birthday and our three-month anniversary. I had imagined asking Nicole to present them on the proper date, but I wanted to see your reaction myself. You asked if you could open the box already. I couldn't resist; I said yes, as long as you waited to wear them. You seemed so pleased. Chuckoy was around while I packed, and I could tell that beneath his quiet way of showing emotion he was sad to see me go. I gave him my Ohio State beanie. I remember laughing afterward that someone should make sure he didn't wear it all day in that heat.

I said goodbye to the people who had opened their home to me. Tita Neth gave me a small pocket Bible in Tagalog. That touched me because we'd talked about faith through the week, finding familiar ground in our Christian beliefs despite the differences in our backgrounds. There were hugs, thanks, and the feeling that I was leaving real friends, not merely people I'd stayed with. Nicole had given me keychains for Bella and Makynna, another kindness I wanted to carry home. And then came a happy surprise: several of the boys and aunties would accompany us all the way to Manila. I wasn't going to lose everyone's company just yet.

We rode toward Manila in an SUV, with family packed around us. You and I sat together in the middle, and you held my arm with a grip I can still almost feel when I look at the photographs. You fell asleep against me more than once. I didn't want to disturb you, so I quietly photographed what I could and spent much of the drive simply watching you. Afterward you told me you'd wanted to ask me to stay one more day but knew I had to return to work. You didn't know I had checked the possibility of extending my flight myself. I wanted to do it. But I had run out of vacation time and had to face reality. I still sometimes wonder what we might have done with one more morning.

The airport goodbye didn't go as I'd imagined. We all planned to wait together as long as possible. But I misunderstood which entrance I needed and went through to check my bag, thinking I would be able to meet everyone again easily. Instead I found myself stuck in a line while you waited elsewhere, both of us worried that the barrier between us might become permanent before I had a chance to say goodbye properly. I waited and watched the minutes go by, then hurried to find the others as soon as I could. I did find you—thank God—but there was a railing between us. We could lean over it and hold each other as best we could, not have the simple embrace I'd wanted. I promised myself I would understand the airport better next time.

I took off my scarlet-and-gray Ohio State cap and put it on Benj. I had brought it knowing that I wanted to give it to him when I left. I told him to look after his mother for me. Then I said goodbye to everyone, kissed you, and finally made myself walk away. I didn't look back because I didn't want you to see the tears. The hardest part was knowing exactly how wonderful these nine days had been and how much I wanted an ordinary tenth day beside you.

Tita San sent me a message thanking me for the time with the family and for coming so far to show you my love. I answered as warmly as I could and promised to return. I bought dried mangoes to take home, had my carry-on inspected because of them, and boarded with my passport firmly in my possession this time. Even after I left the Philippines, we were still reaching for one another through WhatsApp. We managed another video call during my long trip home. It didn't come close to replacing the feeling of your hand on my arm, but it reminded me that our story wasn't ending at the airport.

I didn't leave wondering whether what we had was real. I left knowing. That certainty made the goodbye hurt, but it also gave me something to carry home: the knowledge that these first nine days were not the whole story. They were only the beginning.
