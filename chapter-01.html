The moment the crowd opened

There is a photograph Tita San took while you were waiting for me at the airport. You are smiling in your white cardigan over your black top, your light skirt, your phone in your hand, with my arrival still somewhere in the future. I didn't know you were out there while I was waiting for my luggage. Later, when we talked about what you had been wearing, you sent me that picture. I wondered whether the person you were texting was me. I still wonder. It became one of my favorite photographs of you, and I chose it as my WhatsApp background. I love it because it shows a moment I never got to see with my own eyes: you waiting for me, while I was waiting to find you.

I had spent so much time imagining what that first meeting might feel like. For months, I had known your face and your voice through a screen, and I had already come to associate you with an unusual kind of peace. But there was one enormous question that no amount of messaging could answer: what would it feel like when you were finally standing in front of me?

I barely slept before I left. Bella and Makynna took me to the airport, and I carried my nerves in the quiet way I usually do. I was about to travel farther than I ever had to meet a woman I'd come to love through a screen. My fear wasn't that you would disappoint me. It was that, after all this hope, you might not actually be there. I couldn't know for certain until I saw you.

Of all the things I could have worried about before crossing the Pacific, I never imagined that the thing that might keep me from seeing you would be my own passport. There were four flights between Cincinnati and Manila: Houston, Honolulu, Guam, and then you. In Houston, the overhead compartments were full when I boarded, so the airline checked my carry-on bag. I handed it over without much thought. What I had forgotten—completely forgotten—was that my passport was inside it.

It didn't occur to me again until Guam. I was at the gate, hearing the announcement to have tickets and passports ready for boarding. I checked my pockets, and that was the moment my calm disappeared. The passport wasn't in a pocket. It was traveling through the airport somewhere inside a bag I couldn't reach. I found a gate agent, explained what I'd done, showed him my baggage receipt, and watched him radio the handlers. He was wonderfully calm about it. I was not. He told me the bag would be sent to baggage claim, but that I would have to go back through customs to retrieve it.

I ran. At the baggage carousel I waited, watching for the bag and thinking of my flight boarding without me. Then I heard someone calling for “Passenger Lis-ley.” Nobody ever pronounces Lisle correctly, but I knew immediately she meant me. I grabbed the bag and, in my desperation, even asked whether I could sneak back upstairs the way I'd come. Of course I couldn't. She'd already radioed customs ahead, though. One person after another was trying to help this frantic American get to Manila.

At customs, I learned I still had to complete a declaration for Guam, even though I intended to turn around and leave on the next plane. The woman directing the line showed me a QR code for the declaration app and told me to return to her when I was finished. I stood there filling it out, watching minutes disappear. She let me jump ahead to an agent. When he asked where I would be staying, I explained that I wasn't staying anywhere—I was supposed to be on a flight that was already boarding. He recognized the situation, waved me through, and pointed me toward security.

At the top of the escalators I saw an empty security line. What incredible luck, I thought. And then I waited for my bag at the scanner while an agent seemed to be finishing the chapter of a book. Another agent noticed how nervous I looked, asked where I was going, and confirmed that my Manila flight was almost finished boarding. The bag finally moved—and then turned aside for a hand search. An opened tube of toothpaste needed to be swabbed. I told the officer about my flight; he worked faster, checked it, and returned the bag. I thanked him, zipped everything up, and ran again.

I reached the gate as the agents were closing the door. They swung it open for me and cheered as I made it. I was sweaty, breathless, and more rattled than I care to admit. After almost missing the last connection, I actually slept a little on that final leg. I can laugh at the whole absurd episode now: four flights, a checked carry-on, a missing passport, a Guam declaration, customs, security, toothpaste, and a sprint to the gate. But in that moment there was only one thing in my mind: I am going to see my girl.

I remember the nighttime air when I emerged in Manila. I remember looking, and looking again. The airport was full of people, but what I was hearing around me barely registered. I was looking for one face. I tried to keep my usual calm exterior, but as the seconds passed and I couldn't find you, the fear started to rise. Had I missed you? Were you somewhere else? Was this really going to happen?

Then the crowd opened, and there you were. Everything I had been worrying about seemed to disappear at once. I remember your smile, your shyness, your sweetness. I remember the small bouquet you had made from pastel ribbons. And above everything else, I remember that first embrace. I had hoped for a feeling of peace when I held you. Instead, I found something so warm and complete that I have struggled to describe it ever since. You later told me I looked relieved. I was. More than you could possibly know.

Tita San took a photograph of us at the airport. It became my WhatsApp background, a reminder of the moment you stopped being a person on my phone and became the woman I could actually hold. There was Benj, too, and the strange, lovely reality that I wasn't just meeting you: I was beginning to meet your world.

On the ride from the airport, Tita San sat up front beside the driver. I was in the back with you and Benj. He started between us, and at some point you moved to the middle. There was small talk about my travel and whether I was tired. I was content to listen. The important part was the simple fact of your hand in mine and your body beside me.

Tita San and Benj went home. We went directly to the hotel together. We checked in, settled into the room, and lay down still dressed in the clothes we had worn all day. There was no grand first-night scene. I am not naturally bold about making a first move, and you were shy, too. We held each other lightly. We looked at each other. I gave you the picture frame with its note and the Graeter's chocolates I had brought from home. I wanted to spend the entire night memorizing your eyes, even as exhaustion finally caught up with me. If our first evening sounds quiet, it was. That is part of why it was perfect.

There are no photographs here to stand in for what I remember. Some moments have to live in words.
