The first ordinary morning

Waking up beside you for the first time felt like something I had wanted for a lifetime. Our greeting was ordinary—good morning, how did you sleep?—and that was part of the beauty of it. We had spent months looking at one another through a phone. Now I could simply turn and see you there. After separate showers and breakfast at the hotel restaurant, we began talking about what we needed to accomplish: gift bags, visits with your Taytay family, and groceries for the days ahead in Batangas. It sounded more like the plan for a normal family day than an elaborate vacation itinerary. I liked that immediately.

We walked through the kind of market I wasn't accustomed to at home: closely spaced private stalls, an open-air feeling, and people talking and negotiating around us. We needed three gift bags—one for Benj, one for family in Taytay, and one for the relatives we'd be visiting in Batangas. It was the sort of simple errand I might normally forget. But because I was walking it with you, I remember the surroundings, the little booths, and how much I enjoyed being in the middle of your world.

And then came the tricycle sidecar. I had never ridden in one. I couldn't believe how small the compartment was; my knees seemed to be practically up at my throat, and even ducking my head left me close to the roof. There was barely room for one person my size, let alone the two of us. You fit easily, of course. I was folded up like a piece of camping furniture. It was uncomfortable and funny and wonderful all at once. When I later tried to reconstruct the precise order of our stops, I wasn't certain whether the first ride took us to the family home or whether it came after buying the bags. What I know is how ridiculous that little ride felt and how happy I was to be squeezed in there with you.

Back at the hotel I organized the gifts. I had brought chocolates from home, keychains, candles, magnets, and other little things so I could share a piece of Ohio with your relatives. Benj's gift was especially fun. At the mall later, when we ate together, I arranged the bag so he could notice the LEGO sets without me making a big presentation. He was quiet about it, but I wanted him to know that I had thought about him long before we met.

Meeting the family at your Taytay home was a moment of real culture shock for me. The rooms felt crowded, people lived close together, and there weren't many of the comforts I take for granted. I didn't want my surprise to become judgment. What mattered far more was the kindness with which I was welcomed. I was meeting relatives whose names and relationships I still had to learn, and I was seeing a part of your life I could never have understood from photographs alone. I began to appreciate more deeply the effort you put into looking after the people around you.

After the home visit we met Tita San and Benj and went grocery shopping for Batangas. The mall itself felt quite familiar, much more like a modern American shopping center than the earlier market. We filled the cart with things the family would need for meals and snacks. At one point, Tita San examined bags of what she seemed to think was rice. They were actually brown sugar. When you pointed it out, she laughed at herself, and we all laughed with her. I love that such a small, silly moment stayed with me.

We also met the pastor associated with your school. He was someone you thought of with grandfatherly affection, and I was pleased that he took the time to meet me. You later told me he'd approved of me. I know that might sound like a small thing, but I was trying to understand the people and institutions that mattered to you. I wanted to be welcomed as more than a distant foreign boyfriend visiting for a few days.

We debated whether Benj would come back to the hotel to swim, but in the end he went home with Tita San and the groceries. You and I went to look at the pool. We started out wearing our street clothes and ended up swimming in them. That seemed unusual to me, but I gradually discovered it was perfectly normal for you. The pool was almost empty. You told me you weren't a strong swimmer and kept near the wall, especially toward the deep end. I swam a few lengths while you watched. Then I encouraged you to swim toward me. You did it, a little awkwardly at first, like a baby deer finding its legs, and I thought it was the sweetest thing. We even shared a small kiss in the water. The photographs from that afternoon show the pool and the place where I began to feel less like a man visiting a woman on the other side of the world and more like someone who was simply enjoying a day with his girl.

I had started taking pictures because I was trying to get better at preserving life's moments. Years earlier, after a close friend's death, I realized how few photographs I had of the two of us. I didn't want to repeat that mistake with the people I loved. You and I were making memories I already knew would be precious, and I wanted us to have more than my imperfect recollection to carry home.
