When the music took over

By now, I wasn't trying quite so hard to figure out how I was supposed to behave. I was becoming comfortable enough to let myself be playful. You had already seen my lopsided half-smile, and you took delight in copying it. You mocked my eye rolls, too, always affectionately. I loved your own expressions—the smile, the eye rolls, those little pursed lips that could melt me without a word.

That morning began more slowly than some of the others. The previous evening had caught up with us a little, especially you, and there was no urgency to rush out into the day. We rested. We walked. At one point I remember taking a flower and putting it in your hair. These small gestures are easy to lose when retelling a trip by its big events, yet they are the moments my mind returns to most readily. I wanted you to feel noticed even on the ordinary road to the little shop.

On one walk, we stopped at a neighbor's house and were invited inside. There were new sandals in packages, the sort of unexpected shopping opportunity I never could have planned. We tried some on and left with several pairs, including one for me. I remember being astonished by the price compared with what I might have paid at home. Later I helped Tito Erick clean a chicken for a meal. For me, coming from a family familiar with country life, preparing food that way didn't seem shocking. More importantly, I was glad to be useful. I didn't want to remain a guest who merely sat and watched other people work.

By late afternoon we were getting dressed for an outing I didn't yet fully understand. The neighbor who had sold us the sandals came over to the house, and we squeezed into the tricycle with Tito Erick and Tita Neth. Then we stopped at a place I recognized: the house by the bend in the road where I had met some friendly men on one of our walks. Only now it had been transformed into a celebration. There was a stage, tents, little cabanas, and what seemed like hundreds of people. For a moment I felt like somebody had stopped the music when I entered. People were smiling, waving, and turning to look. I don't naturally seek attention; I usually prefer being supporting cast. But I have to admit that their welcome made me smile.

The banner explained what we were attending: a couple's fiftieth wedding anniversary. That seemed worth celebrating properly. Long tables held the food, and a lively man who had done much of the cooking came over to introduce himself. He disappeared, returned with a bottle and glasses, disappeared again, and brought me yet another plate. Before long I felt I had become his special guest. He wanted to toast with me, and the surprise on people's faces when I finished a glass without the reaction they expected made everyone laugh. This was not the quiet evening I had imagined when we got dressed.

After we ate, the cook guided Tito Erick and me toward a circle of men in front of the stage. I hesitated because it meant leaving you sitting farther back with Tita Neth and the neighbor. You encouraged me to go. You seemed delighted to watch everyone welcoming me, and you took my phone so you could capture some of the evening yourself. The men passed bottles and glasses around, teasing me about how much I could drink. I kept glancing across the circle to see your face. Even with a crowd of new friends around me, I would rather have been sitting beside you.

Then I noticed you on your phone. You were trying to hear over the band, one hand covering your ear, and you walked away. At first I assumed you needed a quieter place to talk. But when I saw you heading down the road, I knew something might be wrong. I excused myself and followed. You had a substantial head start, and the bend in the road kept me from cutting straight across. I walked, jogged, and ran in the heat until I finally caught up to you perhaps half a mile away, breathless and sweating. You turned when you heard me and looked shocked that I had followed. You wanted me to go back and enjoy my new friends. But I wasn't going to let you walk all that way alone. The party was fun; being where you needed me mattered more.

I never completely understood the conversation that had upset you. At home, you found towels and carefully dabbed the sweat from my head and face, the nurturing little gesture I had already come to recognize. Then something else happened in the house, and you came to our room in tears. You didn't want to talk about it yet. I couldn't solve what I didn't understand, and I had to accept that. I held you and let you have the quiet you needed. I've never forgotten the contrast between the noisy celebration and that moment alone with you. It reminded me that the purpose of this trip wasn't simply to collect exciting experiences. It was to learn how to be present when something was difficult, too.

When you felt better, you asked whether I wanted to return to the party. I was happy to go if you were. So we went back, and this time we settled closer together. The band was good—really good—and I enjoyed the mix of English songs, Tagalog songs, and people taking turns at the microphone. One man giving a toast to the anniversary couple apparently included me in his remarks. I didn't understand what he said; I only saw him lift his glass in my direction. I raised mine back. You later explained that he'd been recognizing me, and that small kindness stayed with me. Even the announcer seemed to enjoy drawing attention to the unexpected American guest.

The last set became a kind of live karaoke. Singers came down from the stage, guests joined in, and the band seemed able to play whatever anyone wanted. Eventually that irrepressible cook got me up in front of everyone to dance with him. Me—the man who says he doesn't dance, especially not with other men! You caught the absurdity on video, and it became one of your favorite parts of the whole visit. When I watch it, I don't just see the dancing. I remember the people laughing, the man determined that I should have a wonderful time, and your delight in seeing me let my guard down.

It had been such a surprising day: a slow morning, a flower in your hair, sandals from a neighbor, helping prepare a chicken, a golden-anniversary celebration, concern when you left the crowd, a moment of tears, and then laughter and dancing beneath the lights. The photographs help me recover the order of those things. What I most want to remember, though, is how naturally I kept choosing to be wherever you were.

I was beginning to imagine more than another visit. Seeing daily life, the countryside, your family, and the way you moved through all of it made me wonder what having a place in the Philippines might look like. I wasn't making a final plan. I was noticing that the idea of spending more time here no longer seemed far-fetched. You were changing the way I imagined the future.

And when the noise finally faded, there you still were: the person whose company I wanted most whether I was dancing with strangers or sitting in the quiet.
